🧠 The Golden Mindset

P&C is NOT about math. It is about logic — the science of counting.
Every single problem boils down to one question:

Am I ARRANGING things?  OR  Am I SELECTING things?

If ORDER matters → Permutation  |  If ORDER doesn't matter → Combination

Permutation = ARRANGEMENT

The order matters. A-B-C is different from C-B-A.

🏆 Awarding 1st, 2nd, 3rd rank
🔐 Creating a 4-digit PIN
📸 Seating people in a row

Combination = SELECTION

The order doesn't matter. A-B-C is the same as C-B-A.

👥 Choosing a cricket team
🌸 Picking flowers for a bouquet
🗳️ Forming a committee

The Foundation

Fundamental Principles of Counting

Before any formula, master these two rules. Every P&C problem in existence is built on just these two ideas.

🔗 Rule 1 — Multiplication Rule (The "AND" Rule)

If Task 1 can be done in m ways AND Task 2 can be done in n ways,
then both together = m × n ways.

Multiplication Rule
Total = m × n
Use when tasks happen TOGETHER / ONE AFTER ANOTHER
✈️
Indian Student Analogy — Delhi to Mumbai

There are 3 flights from Delhi to Mumbai (m=3). From Mumbai airport, there are 4 taxi services to your hotel (n=4). Total ways to reach hotel = 3 × 4 = 12 ways.

You need BOTH the flight AND the taxi → Multiply!

💡 Keyword: "AND" → MULTIPLY  |  "followed by" → MULTIPLY

➕ Rule 2 — Addition Rule (The "OR" Rule)

If Job 1 can be done in m ways OR Job 2 can be done in n ways (alternatives — you pick only one),
then total = m + n ways.

Addition Rule
Total = m + n
Use when you choose ONE ALTERNATIVE from multiple options
🍽️
Indian Student Analogy — Canteen Snacks

The canteen has 5 types of samosas and 3 types of kachoris. If you want to eat only ONE snack (samosa OR kachori), you have 5 + 3 = 8 options.

You choose ONE thing — either samosa or kachori → Add!

💡 Keyword: "OR" → ADD  |  "either/alternatively" → ADD

The Building Block

Factorials (!)

What is a Factorial?

A factorial is a shorthand way of writing the product of consecutive natural numbers from 1 to n.

Definition
n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … × 3 × 2 × 1
Read "n factorial". Also written as ⌊n (floor n)
Recursive Property (Very Useful!)
n! = n × (n−1)!
Example: 6! = 6 × 5! = 6 × 120 = 720

Most Common Student Mistake — NEVER Do This!

Factorials CANNOT be distributed over addition:

(2 + 3)! ≠ 2! + 3!

Incorrect: 2! + 3! = 2 + 6 = 8

Correct: (2 + 3)! = 5! = 120

First simplify the bracket, THEN apply factorial.

✅ Solved Example — Simplification
Simplify: 8! / 6!
1
Write out: 8! / 6! = (8 × 7 × 6!) / 6!
2
Cancel 6! from numerator and denominator
3
= 8 × 7 = 56
Answer = 56  ✓

Order Matters

Permutations — The Art of Arrangement

Core Idea: ORDER MATTERS in Permutations

If you arrange Suresh and Ramesh in 2 chairs:

Chair 1: Suresh  |  Chair 2: Ramesh
Chair 1: Ramesh  |  Chair 2: Suresh

These are 2 different permutations because position matters!

Imagine awarding 1st, 2nd, and 3rd ranks from a class of 10 CA students:

This is exactly 10P3 = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720. The formula just automates this logic!

Key Formula — Permutations
ⁿPᵣ = n! / (n − r)!
n = total items available  |  r = items to be arranged
💡
Shortcut — No Need to Expand Full Factorial!

ⁿPᵣ = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … (r terms). Just multiply r terms starting from n going down.

Example: 8P3 = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336    (3 terms starting from 8)

🟢 Easy — Evaluate 5P3
1
5P3 = 5! / (5−3)! = 5! / 2!
2
= (5 × 4 × 3 × 2!) / 2! = 5 × 4 × 3
5P3 = 60

How many 3-letter words can be formed from the letters of "SQUARE"?
1
"SQUARE" has 6 distinct letters: S, Q, U, A, R, E

Arrange 4 brothers and 3 sisters in a row so that all 3 sisters always sit together.
1

2

Special Cases

Special Permutations

🔄 Circular Permutations

When people sit around a round table, there is no fixed "first" position. We fix one person as reference and arrange the rest.

Round Table
(n − 1)!
Fix 1 person, arrange remaining (n−1)
🍽️
Why (n-1)! and not n!?

In a circle, rotating everyone one seat gives the SAME arrangement. So we "fix" one person to remove this duplication. 6 people at round table = 5! = 120 (not 6!).

📿 Necklaces & Garlands

When making a necklace, you can flip it over — so clockwise and anti-clockwise arrangements look identical.

Necklace / Garland
½ × (n − 1)!
Divide by 2 to remove flip duplicates
💡
Remember

Round table (can't flip) → (n-1)!    Necklace (can flip) → ½(n-1)!

🔁 Permutations with Identical Objects

When some objects are exactly alike, swapping identical objects doesn't create a new arrangement. We divide by the factorials of repeated items to avoid overcounting.

With Identical Objects
n! / (p₁! × p₂! × p₃! × …)
p₁, p₂, p₃ = count of each set of identical objects
Example — Letters of "COMMITTEE"
In how many ways can letters of "COMMITTEE" be arranged?
1
COMMITTEE = 9 letters: C, O, M, M, I, T, T, E, E → M appears 2×, T appears 2×, E appears 2×
2
Ways = 9! / (2! × 2! × 2!) = 362880 / 8
= 45,360 arrangements

🔢 Permutations WITH Repetition

When you can reuse the same item (like digits in a PIN), each position has n choices every time.

Repetition Allowed
Total = nʳ
n = items to choose from, r = positions to fill
🔐
PIN Analogy

A 4-digit PIN using digits 0-9 (repetition allowed): 10⁴ = 10,000 possible PINs. That's why banks use 4-digit PINs — enough variety to be secure!

Order Does NOT Matter

Combinations — The Art of Selection

Core Idea: ORDER DOESN'T MATTER in Combinations

You are selecting a cricket team of 11 from 15 players. If Virat and Rohit are both selected, it doesn't matter which one was "chosen first" — they're in the team!

Permutation: {A,B} ≠ {B,A} → 2 different arrangements
vs
Combination: {A,B} = {B,A} → Same team!
🔑 nCr = nPr / r!  →  Selection = Arrangement ÷ Internal order
Key Formula — Combinations
ⁿCᵣ = n! / (r! × (n − r)!)
Also: ⁿCᵣ = ⁿPᵣ / r!  |  n = total, r = items to select
🟢 Easy — Form a committee of 3 from 12 people
In how many ways can a committee of 3 be formed from 12 people?
1
Order doesn't matter in a committee → use Combination
2
12C3 = 12! / (3! × 9!) = (12 × 11 × 10) / (3 × 2 × 1)
3
= 1320 / 6 = 220
220 ways to form the committee
🟡 Moderate — Mixed Committee (with conditions)
A committee of 7 is to be formed from 6 CAs, 4 Economists, and 5 Cost Accountants. The committee must have exactly 3 CAs, 2 Economists, and 2 Cost Accountants.
1
Select 3 from 6 CAs: 6C3 = 20
2
Select 2 from 4 Economists: 4C2 = 6
3
Select 2 from 5 Cost Accountants: 5C2 = 10
4
All three selections happen AND → Multiply: 20 × 6 × 10
Total = 1,200 ways
🟣 CA Exam Level — Always/Never Together
A team of 5 is selected from 8 people. Person A must always be included, and Person B must never be included.
1
Person A always included: Fix A in the team. Now select 4 more from remaining 6 people (excluding B).
2
Remaining people = 8 − 1 (A) − 1 (B) = 6 people
3
Select 4 from 6: 6C4 = 6C2 = 15
15 ways to form the team

Time-Saving Shortcuts

Important Properties of Combinations

nCr = nC(n−r) — Complementary

Selecting r items is the same as leaving out (n−r) items!

10C8 = 10C2 = 45
Always calculate the smaller one! Much faster in exams.

Pascal's Identity

Selecting r from n+1 = two ways from n:

nCr + nC(r−1) = (n+1)Cr
Used in algebraic expansions and proofs

nC0 = nCn = 1

Selecting none OR selecting all = only 1 way each.

10C0 = 1    10C10 = 1
Zero things or all things — just one way

One or More Selections

Total ways to select at least 1 item from n distinct items:

2ⁿ − 1
2ⁿ = all possible selections (including none). Subtract 1 for "select none" case.

Always Included / Must Be Selected

If a specific person MUST be in the team of r (selected from n), fix them and choose the rest:

Always Included Formula
(n−1)C(r−1)
Fix the special person, then choose r−1 more from the remaining n−1 people

CA Exam Mastery

Exam Strategies & Short Tricks

🎯 Strategy 1 — Handle Restrictions FIRST

If the problem says "the President must be included" or "vowels must be together," fix those special items first, then arrange the rest freely.

🚫 Strategy 2 — "Never Together" Shortcut

Never Together = Total Ways − Always Together

It's easier to count what you DON'T want and subtract it!

🔲 Strategy 3 — Gap Method

When "no two X can be adjacent": arrange the non-X items first, then place X items in the gaps created between them.

_ B _ B _ B _ B _ B _ B _ (n boys create n+1 gaps)

⚡ Strategy 4 — Use Complementary Combination

Always compute nCr using the smaller value of r.

10C8 → use 10C2 instead. Same answer, half the work!

🧮 Strategy 5 — Cancel Factorials Intelligently

For 10C3, don't calculate 10! and 7! separately:

10C3 = (10 × 9 × 8) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 120

Only compute r terms in numerator and r! in denominator.

🏷️ Strategy 6 — Identify Type Instantly

Rank / Password / Arrange / Word / Seat in row → Permutation

Team / Committee / Group / Choose / Select → Combination

⚠️
Common Traps in CA Exams
  • Round table ≠ Row: Don't use n! for circular arrangement. Use (n−1)!
  • Necklace ≠ Round table: Necklace can be flipped, so use ½(n−1)!
  • Digits with 0: If forming numbers, 0 cannot be the first digit — subtract those cases!
  • "At least" questions: Use Total − (None) instead of adding each case separately.

Practice Section

Try each question yourself first, then click "Show Answer" to reveal the solution.

🟢 Section A — Basic Questions
Q1. Evaluate 10C2.
10C2 = 10C8 = (10×9)/(2×1) = 90/2 = 45
Q2. In how many ways can 5 persons stand in a row for a photo?
All 5 arranged in a row = 5P5 = 5! = 5×4×3×2×1 = 120 ways
Q3. Evaluate 9P0.
9P0 = 9! / (9-0)! = 9!/9! = 1  (Zero items arranged = 1 way, the "do nothing" way)
Q4. Simplify: 12! / (10! × 2!)
= (12 × 11 × 10!) / (10! × 2) = (12 × 11) / 2 = 132/2 = 66
🟡 Section B — Moderate Questions
Q5. How many ways can 4 red balls, 3 yellow balls, and 2 green balls be arranged in a row (same colour balls are identical)?
Total = 9 balls, identical objects formula:
9! / (4! × 3! × 2!) = 362880 / (24 × 6 × 2) = 362880 / 288 = 1,260 ways
Q6. How many ways can 8 people sit at a round table if 2 particular people must always sit together?
Step 1: Treat the 2 people as 1 unit → 7 units total
Step 2: Arrange 7 units in circle = (7-1)! = 6! = 720
Step 3: 2 people within the unit can swap = 2! = 2
Total = 6! × 2 = 720 × 2 = 1,440 ways
Q7. From 5 consonants and 4 vowels, how many words of 3 consonants and 2 vowels can be formed?
Step 1: Select 3 consonants from 5: 5C3 = 10
Step 2: Select 2 vowels from 4: 4C2 = 6
Step 3: Arrange the 5 chosen letters in a word: 5! = 120
Total = 10 × 6 × 120 = 7,200 words
Q8. How many numbers between 100 and 1000 can be formed with digits 0,1,2,3,4,5 (no repetition)?
3-digit numbers, first digit ≠ 0.
Hundreds place: 5 choices (1,2,3,4,5)
Tens place: 5 remaining digits (including 0)
Units place: 4 remaining digits
Total = 5 × 5 × 4 = 100 numbers
🟣 Section C — Previous CA Foundation Level
Q9. If nP4 = 12 × nP2, find n.
Expand: n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3) = 12 × n(n-1)
Cancel n(n-1) from both sides: (n-2)(n-3) = 12
Since 4 × 3 = 12, we have (n-2) = 4 → n = 6
Verify: 6P4 = 360, 12 × 6P2 = 12 × 30 = 360 ✓
Q10. How many ways can letters of "COMPUTER" be rearranged (excluding the original word)?
COMPUTER = 8 distinct letters
Total arrangements = 8! = 40,320
Subtract 1 (the original word): 40,320 − 1 = 40,319 ways
Q11. In how many ways can the word "ASSASSINATION" be arranged?
ASSASSINATION = 13 letters: A×3, S×4, I×2, N×2, T×1, O×1
Ways = 13! / (3! × 4! × 2! × 2! × 1! × 1!)
= 6,227,020,800 / (6 × 24 × 2 × 2) = 6,227,020,800 / 576 = 10,810,800 ways
Q12. If nC12 = nC8, find nC17 and nC20.
Since nCr = nC(n-r), if nC12 = nC8, then 12 + 8 = n → n = 20
20C17 = 20C3 = (20×19×18)/(3×2×1) = 6840/6 = 1,140
20C20 = 1
Q13. A polygon has 35 diagonals. Find the number of its sides.
Diagonals in n-sided polygon = nC2 − n = n(n-1)/2 − n
Set equal to 35: n(n-1)/2 − n = 35
n(n-1) − 2n = 70 → n² − 3n − 70 = 0
(n-10)(n+7) = 0 → n = 10 (positive value)
The polygon has 10 sides (Decagon)
